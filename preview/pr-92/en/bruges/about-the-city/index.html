This is Bruges

Bruges is the capital of West Flanders, a small city of canals, brick houses and medieval churches in the north-west of Belgium. From the 13th to the 15th century it was one of the great trading cities of northern Europe; when its channel to the sea silted up, the money left and the city slept for centuries, which is why so much of its medieval centre survives. That centre is on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

The city

Bruges is the best-kept medieval city of Flanders. Its centre still lies inside the oval of its medieval walls, now a ring of canals and parks, and the canals that once brought ships to its markets still wind between the houses. Most of the buildings are of dark red brick with stepped gables, and those that look medieval but are too neat were often rebuilt in the 19th century, when the city rediscovered its past.

The centre, the part you ride through in Instant Bruges, turns around two squares a few steps apart: the Markt, with the Belfry, and the Burg, with the City Hall and the Basilica of the Holy Blood. From the Burg, lanes lead south to the canals at the Rozenhoedkaai and the Dijver, then on past the Church of Our Lady to the Begijnhof and the Minnewater. North of the Markt lies the old inner harbour around the Jan van Eyckplein.

Where it lies

Bruges lies in the north-west of Belgium, in the Flemish Region, about 15 kilometres from the North Sea coast. It is the capital of the province of West Flanders. Ghent lies about 45 kilometres to the south-east and Brussels about 90 kilometres away.

Canals link Bruges to Ghent, to Ostend and to its own seaport, Zeebrugge, on the coast to the north. The land around the city is flat polder and farmland, and the climate is mild and wet, with sea winds all year.

History

A harbour on the Zwin

In the 9th century the counts of Flanders built a fortress on the spot that is still called the Burg, and a trading town grew around it. A storm flood in 1134 cut a deep inlet, the Zwin, from the sea towards Bruges, and the city built outports along it at Damme and Sluis. Ships brought English wool for the Flemish weavers and carried away their cloth.

By the 14th century Bruges was the great market of north-west Europe. Italian bankers, German merchants of the Hanseatic League and traders from Spain and Portugal kept houses here; merchants met in front of the inn of the Van der Beurse family, which gave its name to the bourse. In the night of 18 May 1302 the craftsmen of Bruges killed the French garrison in the Bruges Matins, and two months later the Flemish army beat the French knights at the Battle of the Golden Spurs.

The Burgundian court

In the 15th century the dukes of Burgundy, who ruled Flanders, often held court in Bruges. Duke Philip the Good founded the Order of the Golden Fleece here in 1430, at the celebrations of his marriage to Isabella of Portugal. Jan van Eyck, his court painter, lived in Bruges until his death in 1441, and Hans Memling worked here a generation later.

The good years ended quickly. The Zwin silted up, and big ships could no longer reach the city. Mary of Burgundy died after a fall from her horse in 1482, and in 1488 the townspeople held her widower, Maximilian of Austria, prisoner on the Markt for months. Once free, he moved the foreign merchants to Antwerp, and Bruges sank into four centuries of poverty.

The sleeping city wakes

Because nobody had the money to tear the old houses down, the medieval city survived. In the 19th century British visitors and Flemish architects rediscovered it and restored, and sometimes reinvented, its Gothic fronts. Georges Rodenbach’s novel Bruges-la-Morte of 1892 made the grey, silent city famous across Europe.

In 1907 Bruges opened a new seaport on the coast at Zeebrugge, and the city came through both world wars without great damage. Since 1949 it has been home to the College of Europe. UNESCO listed its historic centre in 2000, and in 2002 it was the European Capital of Culture.

Bruges today

Bruges lives largely from tourism: millions of visitors a year come to a centre where about twenty thousand people live. The city council limits the tour boats, the horse carriages and the cruise-ship day trips to keep the old town liveable. Its port at Zeebrugge, merged with Antwerp’s into the Port of Antwerp-Bruges in 2022, handles cars, containers and gas.

On Ascension Day the Procession of the Holy Blood winds through the centre, as it has for centuries. The Concertgebouw on ’t Zand, the College of Europe and the Saturday market keep the city busy beyond the tourist season.
