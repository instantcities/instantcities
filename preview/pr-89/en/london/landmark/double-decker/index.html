Red double-decker

The red two-storey buses that have carried London since the days of the horse bus.

Play London Watch the tour

Photo: 0x010C, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

London has run double-decker buses since horse-drawn omnibuses put seats on their roofs in the 19th century. The Routemaster of 1956, with its open platform at the back, became the city’s emblem.

Today’s buses are still red and still two storeys high. From the front seats upstairs you get the best view in town for the price of a bus fare. Ride through London in 3D ›

More in London

Big BenThe great bell in the clock tower of the Houses of Parliament, whose tower is now the Elizabeth Tower. Houses of ParliamentThe Palace of Westminster on the Thames, rebuilt in Gothic style after the fire of 1834. Westminster AbbeyThe church where English and British monarchs have been crowned since 1066. Trafalgar SquareNelson on his column, four bronze lions and the National Gallery along the north side. National GalleryBritain’s collection of European paintings, free to visit, along the north side of Trafalgar Square. Downing StreetNumber 10, the home of British prime ministers since 1735, behind black gates off Whitehall. Horse GuardsMounted troopers of the Household Cavalry stand sentry on Whitehall every day. London EyeThe giant observation wheel across the Thames, built for the year 2000. Westminster BridgeThe green bridge over the Thames beside Big Ben, painted the colour of the House of Commons’ benches. British MuseumThe world’s first national public museum, in Bloomsbury, with the Rosetta Stone and the Parthenon’s marbles. Black cabLondon’s taxis, tall enough for a man in a top hat, driven by cabbies who know every street by heart. Red phone boxThe red cast-iron telephone kiosks of Giles Gilbert Scott, from the 1920s and 1930s. Changing of the GuardThe ceremony in which the King’s Guard hands over to the new guard, in red tunics and bearskins.
