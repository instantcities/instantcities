Kouter

Ghent’s smartest square, once a field and a place for parades and archers’ feasts. There has been a flower market here every Sunday since the 18th century, with a brass band in the bandstand. The blue kiosk serves a drink to go with it.

The Kouter is Ghent’s grand, elegant square, on the south side of the old centre, a few minutes from the Veldstraat. It is broad and bright, ringed by stately 18th- and 19th-century mansions, banks and the Handelsbeurs concert hall. In the middle stands a graceful bandstand, and at the edge the well-known blue kiosk, where you can get a drink and a bite.

Every Sunday morning the Kouter becomes a sea of flowers and plants: the flower market is a Ghent tradition centuries old. Locals come to buy their bouquet or houseplant, drink a glass of cava or white wine with oysters at the kiosk, and listen to the band playing in the bandstand. It is one of the cosiest moments of the Ghent week.

On weekdays the Kouter is quieter, a place to cross on the way to Opera Ghent just behind the square, or to enjoy the sun on a bench for a moment. Visit it in 3D ›

History

A ‘kouter’ is an old Flemish word for open, shared farmland. In the early Middle Ages this square was indeed fields just outside the city. When Ghent grew and the area came to lie within the walls, the big open space remained.

In the late Middle Ages the Kouter became the square for parades, musters, tournaments and the festivities of the archers’ guilds, who practised here with their crossbows and longbows. Public ceremonies were held here too. In the 18th century it became the square of Ghent’s nobility and wealthy citizens, who had their mansions built around it.

The flower market began in the 18th century and has been there ever since. Ghent is a true city of flowers: the surrounding area is known for ornamental horticulture, with begonias, azaleas and more, and the Ghent Floralies have been one of the world’s largest flower shows since the 19th century. In the 19th century came the bandstand and, just behind the square, the Opera; with the Handelsbeurs concert hall they make the Kouter a cultural centre.

Going to see it

The Kouter is a public square and always open to all. The flower market is on Sunday mornings, roughly from 7 am to 1 pm, and it is at its liveliest between 10 and 12. It is an easy walk from the Korenmarkt along the Veldstraat, or take the tram, which stops close by.

Combine a visit with a stroll down the Veldstraat, Ghent’s main shopping street, or a performance at the Opera or the Handelsbeurs.

More in Ghent

St Bavo’s CathedralBuilt from the 13th to the 16th century, with an 89 m tower. Inside hangs the Van Eyck brothers’ Adoration of the Mystic Lamb (1432), one of the most famous and most stolen paintings in the world. Its Just Judges panel, stolen in 1934, has never been found. BelfryThe town’s 91 m tower, begun in 1313. Ghent kept its charters of rights here, and watchmen looked out for fire and enemies. A gilded dragon keeps watch on top. With the Cloth Hall beside it, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. GravensteenThe castle of the Counts of Flanders, built in 1180 by Philip of Alsace. It later served as a court, a prison and even a cotton mill, and got its battlements back around 1900. In 1949 students occupied it, in protest against the price of beer going up. Graslei and KorenleiGhent’s medieval port, on both banks of the Leie. Boatmen unloaded grain here and the guilds built their houses along the quay. The Koornstapelhuis, the grey Romanesque grain store from around 1200, is one of the oldest houses in town. St Michael’s BridgeBuilt at the start of the 20th century, just before the 1913 World Fair. Look east to see Ghent’s three towers in a row: St Nicholas’, the Belfry and St Bavo’s. Behind you stands St Michael’s Church, whose tower was never finished. St Nicholas’ ChurchThe merchants’ church, from the 13th century, in blue-grey Tournai stone: Scheldt Gothic. Its tower sits right on the middle of the roof and doubled as a lookout, long before the Belfry stood. Today it also hosts concerts and exhibitions. City HallA city hall in two styles: ornate late Gothic on the Hoogpoort, sober Renaissance on the Botermarkt. Running out of money and the wars of religion kept halting the building work. The Pacification of Ghent, a peace between the Low Countries’ provinces, was signed here in 1576. VrijdagmarktThe square of markets, feasts and uprisings, where the Counts of Flanders were sworn in. In the middle Jacob van Artevelde, the brewer who led Ghent in 1338, points the way. Across the square stands Ons Huis, the big home of the socialist workers’ movement. Groot VleeshuisThe butchers’ covered meat hall, built from 1406 to 1419. Hams still hang drying under its impressive wooden roof. Today you can buy and taste East Flemish regional produce here. Graffiti StreetThe Werregarenstraatje: since the 1990s anyone may spray paint here, legally. The walls get a fresh layer of paint almost every week, so no two visits look the same. VooruitThe party hall of the socialist co-operative Vooruit, opened in 1914: workers came here to dance, for theatre and concerts, and to eat in the café. Artists rescued it from decay in the 1980s. Today it is the arts centre VIERNULVIER. St Peter’s AbbeyThe abbey on the Blandijnberg hill was founded in the 7th century, by Saint Amand as the story goes, and grew into one of the richest in Flanders. The baroque church with its dome (1629-1720) is by Pieter Huyssens. The French dissolved the abbey in 1796; today it is an exhibition hall, with an abbey garden of vines and ruins behind its walls. Gent ZuidUntil 1928 the Zuidstation, Ghent’s first railway station, stood here. Today it is De Lijn’s hub: most city buses start and end here, and the trams pass on their way to the centre and Sint-Pieters. BlaarmeersenGhent’s sports and leisure park, by the Watersportbaan. On hot days the beach on the swimming lake is packed, and joggers run their laps round the 2.3 km rowing course. There is also a campsite, an athletics track and beach volleyball. MammelokkerLook above the door of this little house against the Cloth Hall: a young woman giving an old man her breast. It is the Roman legend of Pero, who nursed her father Cimon when he was left to starve in prison. When it came out, he was set free. The warders of the town prison, which was in the Cloth Hall’s cellars from 1742 to 1902, lived here. Mammelokker is Ghent dialect for breast-sucker. The relief dates from 1741, recarved by Jan Anteunis in 1962. Karel SabbebergGhent’s only mountain, some 30 metres high, and man-made. Until 2003 Ghent skied here on brush mats. In 2019 it was named after the ultrarunner Karel Sabbe, who ran up and down it thousands of times in training. Since 2009 a lookout tower on top looks out over the city. CuberdonsPurple cone-shaped sweets with a runny raspberry heart, sold from little carts on the Groentenmarkt. The tramsDe Lijn’s white and yellow trams, ringing their bells through the narrow streets of the old town. Gentse FeestenTen days every July when the whole old town becomes one of the largest street festivals in Europe. The cycling citySince 2017 cars can no longer drive straight across the centre, and bikes have taken over the streets.
