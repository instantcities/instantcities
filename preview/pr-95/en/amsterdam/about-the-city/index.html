This is Amsterdam

Amsterdam is the capital of the Netherlands, a city of canals, narrow brick houses and bicycles. It began as a fishing village at a dam in the river Amstel and, in the 17th century, became the richest trading port in Europe. The canal ring that its merchants dug in that Golden Age still holds the city together today.

The city

Amsterdam lies on the IJ, a former arm of the sea, in a land of reclaimed polders, much of it below sea level. Its houses stand on wooden piles driven through the soft peat into the sand underneath, and its old centre is a horseshoe of canals around the Dam, the square where the city began.

Instant Amsterdam takes you through this centre. From Centraal Station the Damrak leads south to the Dam, with the Royal Palace and the Nieuwe Kerk on its west side. The Rokin and the Kalverstraat continue south to the Spui and the Munt tower, while to the west the Singel, the Herengracht, the Keizersgracht and the Prinsengracht curve around the old town, past the Westerkerk and the Anne Frank House. East of the Dam lie the Oude Kerk, the Red Light District and the Nieuwmarkt.

Where it lies

Amsterdam lies in the province of North Holland, in the west of the Netherlands, about 25 kilometres from the North Sea, to which the North Sea Canal links its port. The IJ separates the old city from Amsterdam-Noord, and the ferries across it from behind Centraal Station are free.

The city has about 165 canals and well over a thousand bridges, more than Venice. Its climate is mild and maritime: cool summers, damp winters, frequent wind and rain at any time of the year.

History

A dam in the Amstel

In the 13th century the people living at the mouth of the Amstel built a dam across the river to keep out the water of the IJ. A settlement of fishermen and traders grew up around it, and in 1275 Count Floris V of Holland freed them from paying tolls in his county, the oldest written mention of Amsterdam. The town received its city rights around 1300.

In 1345, the story goes, a dying man could not keep down the communion host, which was thrown into the fire and found unburnt. The Miracle of Amsterdam made the town a place of pilgrimage. Its merchants grew rich on beer, herring and the grain trade with the Baltic, and the Oude Kerk, the city’s oldest building, grew with them.

The Golden Age

During the Dutch revolt against Spain, Amsterdam went over to the Protestant side in 1578. When Spanish troops took Antwerp in 1585, thousands of merchants, craftsmen and Jewish families from Spain and Portugal moved north, and Amsterdam took Antwerp’s place as the trading capital of Europe. In 1602 merchants founded the Dutch East India Company, and the shares that people traded in it in Amsterdam made the city home to one of the world’s first stock exchanges.

From 1613 the city council had the canal ring dug, with the Herengracht, the Keizersgracht and the Prinsengracht, and sold the plots along them to wealthy merchants. On the Dam, Jacob van Campen built a new town hall, begun in 1648, the largest in Europe at the time. Rembrandt painted his Night Watch in the city in 1642.

Capital of the Netherlands

Wars with England and France ended the Golden Age, and French troops occupied the country in 1795. In 1808 Louis Napoleon, king of Holland, turned the town hall into his royal palace, and after the French left, the new kingdom named Amsterdam its capital, though the government stayed in The Hague. In the 19th century the North Sea Canal opened a new route to the sea, and the Rijksmuseum and Centraal Station rose on the edges of the old city.

German troops occupied Amsterdam from 1940 to 1945. In February 1941 its workers struck against the persecution of their Jewish neighbours, but the occupiers deported and murdered most of the city’s Jews, among them Anne Frank, who had hidden with her family in a canal house on the Prinsengracht from 1942 to 1944. After the war the city hosted hippies, squatters and protests, and in 2010 UNESCO listed the canal ring as World Heritage.

Amsterdam today

Amsterdam is the capital of the Netherlands, though the king, the government and parliament sit in The Hague. Its economy runs on finance and business services, many of them in the Zuidas district, on technology and creative firms, on the port and on Schiphol, one of Europe’s busiest airports. The Rijksmuseum, the Van Gogh Museum and the Anne Frank House draw visitors from all over the world, and the city council has been trying to limit the crowds in the old centre.

Amsterdammers make many of their trips by bike, on a network of red cycle paths. On King’s Day, 27 April, the canals fill with boats and the whole city turns orange. The football club Ajax plays at the Johan Cruijff ArenA, in the south-east of the city.
