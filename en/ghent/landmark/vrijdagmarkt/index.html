Vrijdagmarkt

The square of markets, feasts and uprisings, where the Counts of Flanders were sworn in. In the middle Jacob van Artevelde, the brewer who led Ghent in 1338, points the way. Across the square stands Ons Huis, the big home of the socialist workers’ movement.

The Vrijdagmarkt is one of the largest and oldest squares in Ghent, and for centuries it was the political heart of the city. It is a broad, open square, ringed by houses with stepped gables, Baroque fronts and big 19th-century buildings, with café terraces all around. In the middle stands the bronze statue of Jacob van Artevelde, pointing west with his arm outstretched: towards England, the country with which he made a crucial deal.

There is a lot to discover around the square. The large Ons Huis (Our House), with its rich, colourful front full of inscriptions, was the headquarters of the socialist labour movement. On the corner stands the Toreken, the tanners’ guild house with its slender tower, now a poetry centre. And at the Dulle Griet café you drink beer from a ‘Max’, a glass of more than a litre, for which you have to hand over one of your shoes as a deposit; it is then hoisted in a basket to the ceiling.

Right next to the square, on the Grootkanonplein, is the Dulle Griet (Mad Meg), a huge red wrought-iron cannon from the 15th century, one of the largest medieval cannons to survive. It was never really used in battle, but it is a much-loved symbol of the city. Visit it in 3D ›

History

The name says it all: a market has been held here every Friday (vrijdag) since the Middle Ages. But the Vrijdagmarkt was much more than a market square. This is where the Counts of Flanders were sworn in on their ceremonial entry and promised to respect the city’s rights. Here the guilds gathered, tournaments and feasts were held, and executions too.

In the 14th century Ghent was caught in the Hundred Years’ War between France and England. Ghent lived from cloth-making and needed English wool. In 1338 the wealthy merchant Jacob van Artevelde took charge of the city and kept Flanders neutral, so the wool kept coming. His power did not last: in 1345 he was murdered in a popular uprising. That same year the square saw ‘Evil Monday’, a bloody street battle between the weavers and the fullers that left hundreds dead.

In the 19th century, as Ghent became an industrial city, the Vrijdagmarkt became the square of the labour movement. Around 1900 the Vooruit cooperative built Ons Huis here, with shops, a café and meeting halls for the workers. The statue of Van Artevelde, by Pieter De Vigne, arrived in 1863. Today the square is above all a friendly place of terraces, with an underground car park beneath it.

Going to see it

The Vrijdagmarkt is a public square, always open to all, five minutes’ walk from the Gravensteen and the Town Hall. There is still a market on Friday mornings, and often on Saturdays too. Around the square you can eat and drink on plenty of terraces.

Combine your visit with a walk to the Great Butchers’ Hall and the Patershol, or across the bridge to the Dulle Griet cannon and the Kraanlei.

More in Ghent

St Bavo’s CathedralBuilt from the 13th to the 16th century, with an 89 m tower. Inside hangs the Van Eyck brothers’ Adoration of the Mystic Lamb (1432), one of the most famous and most stolen paintings in the world. Its Just Judges panel, stolen in 1934, has never been found. BelfryThe town’s 91 m tower, begun in 1313. Ghent kept its charters of rights here, and watchmen looked out for fire and enemies. A gilded dragon keeps watch on top. With the Cloth Hall beside it, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. GravensteenThe castle of the Counts of Flanders, built in 1180 by Philip of Alsace. It later served as a court, a prison and even a cotton mill, and got its battlements back around 1900. In 1949 students occupied it, in protest against the price of beer going up. Graslei and KorenleiGhent’s medieval port, on both banks of the Leie. Boatmen unloaded grain here and the guilds built their houses along the quay. The Koornstapelhuis, the grey Romanesque grain store from around 1200, is one of the oldest houses in town. St Michael’s BridgeBuilt at the start of the 20th century, just before the 1913 World Fair. Look east to see Ghent’s three towers in a row: St Nicholas’, the Belfry and St Bavo’s. Behind you stands St Michael’s Church, whose tower was never finished. St Nicholas’ ChurchThe merchants’ church, from the 13th century, in blue-grey Tournai stone: Scheldt Gothic. Its tower sits right on the middle of the roof and doubled as a lookout, long before the Belfry stood. Today it also hosts concerts and exhibitions. City HallA city hall in two styles: ornate late Gothic on the Hoogpoort, sober Renaissance on the Botermarkt. Running out of money and the wars of religion kept halting the building work. The Pacification of Ghent, a peace between the Low Countries’ provinces, was signed here in 1576. Groot VleeshuisThe butchers’ covered meat hall, built from 1406 to 1419. Hams still hang drying under its impressive wooden roof. Today you can buy and taste East Flemish regional produce here. Graffiti StreetThe Werregarenstraatje: since the 1990s anyone may spray paint here, legally. The walls get a fresh layer of paint almost every week, so no two visits look the same. KouterGhent’s smartest square, once a field and a place for parades and archers’ feasts. There has been a flower market here every Sunday since the 18th century, with a brass band in the bandstand. The blue kiosk serves a drink to go with it. VooruitThe party hall of the socialist co-operative Vooruit, opened in 1914: workers came here to dance, for theatre and concerts, and to eat in the café. Artists rescued it from decay in the 1980s. Today it is the arts centre VIERNULVIER. St Peter’s AbbeyThe abbey on the Blandijnberg hill was founded in the 7th century, by Saint Amand as the story goes, and grew into one of the richest in Flanders. The baroque church with its dome (1629-1720) is by Pieter Huyssens. The French dissolved the abbey in 1796; today it is an exhibition hall, with an abbey garden of vines and ruins behind its walls. Gent ZuidUntil 1928 the Zuidstation, Ghent’s first railway station, stood here. Today it is De Lijn’s hub: most city buses start and end here, and the trams pass on their way to the centre and Sint-Pieters. BlaarmeersenGhent’s sports and leisure park, by the Watersportbaan. On hot days the beach on the swimming lake is packed, and joggers run their laps round the 2.3 km rowing course. There is also a campsite, an athletics track and beach volleyball. MammelokkerLook above the door of this little house against the Cloth Hall: a young woman giving an old man her breast. It is the Roman legend of Pero, who nursed her father Cimon when he was left to starve in prison. When it came out, he was set free. The warders of the town prison, which was in the Cloth Hall’s cellars from 1742 to 1902, lived here. Mammelokker is Ghent dialect for breast-sucker. The relief dates from 1741, recarved by Jan Anteunis in 1962. Karel SabbebergGhent’s only mountain, some 30 metres high, and man-made. Until 2003 Ghent skied here on brush mats. In 2019 it was named after the ultrarunner Karel Sabbe, who ran up and down it thousands of times in training. Since 2009 a lookout tower on top looks out over the city. CuberdonsPurple cone-shaped sweets with a runny raspberry heart, sold from little carts on the Groentenmarkt. The tramsDe Lijn’s white and yellow trams, ringing their bells through the narrow streets of the old town. Gentse FeestenTen days every July when the whole old town becomes one of the largest street festivals in Europe. The cycling citySince 2017 cars can no longer drive straight across the centre, and bikes have taken over the streets.
