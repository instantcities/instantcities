This is Madrid

Madrid is the capital of Spain and its largest city, high on the plateau in the middle of the country. Philip II made it the seat of his court in 1561, and the kings who followed left it a royal palace, the arcaded Plaza Mayor and the Prado, one of the great art museums of the world. Today Madrid is also known for its late nights, its tapas bars and its football.

The city

Madrid lies about 650 metres above sea level, almost in the geographical centre of Spain, and all the country’s main roads are measured from a plaque in the Puerta del Sol. It is a young capital by European standards: a small town until the king’s court arrived in the 16th century, it grew around the palace into a city of grand avenues, museums and parks.

The old town, the part you ride through in Instant Madrid, is the Madrid de los Austrias, the city of the Habsburg kings. From the Puerta del Sol the Calle Mayor runs west past the Plaza Mayor and the Plaza de la Villa towards the Royal Palace and the cathedral on the bluff above the river. The Gran Vía crosses the north of the centre on its way to the Plaza de España, La Latina and its taverns lie to the south, and the Barrio de las Letras, the quarter of the writers, lies to the east.

Where it lies

Madrid lies in the middle of the Iberian Peninsula, on the high plateau of the Meseta, and is the capital of the autonomous community of the same name. The Sierra de Guadarrama, often white with snow in winter, rises some 50 kilometres to the north-west. The Manzanares, a small river, flows past the west side of the old town; its banks, where the ring road now runs in tunnels, became the long park of Madrid Río in 2011.

The climate is dry, with hot summers and cold, sunny winters. The great parks, the Retiro in the east and the Casa de Campo across the river, give the city its green spaces.

History

Mayrit and the Castilian town

In the 9th century Muhammad I, the emir of Córdoba, built a fortress on the bluff above the Manzanares to guard the road to Toledo against the Christian kingdoms of the north. The Arabs called the place Mayrit, after its streams; a stretch of its wall still stands below the cathedral, by the Cuesta de la Vega.

Alfonso VI of Castile took Madrid in 1085, around the time that he took Toledo. For almost five centuries it remained a modest Castilian town of farmers and craftsmen, where the kings sometimes stayed and the parliament, the Cortes, sometimes met. Its patron saint, Isidore, was a farmhand who worked the fields outside the town.

The court of the kings

In 1561 Philip II moved his court to Madrid, and apart from a few years in Valladolid at the start of the next century it has stayed there ever since. The town grew fast. In the 17th century, Spain’s Golden Age, Cervantes, Lope de Vega and Quevedo wrote in the streets east of the Puerta del Sol, Velázquez painted for the king, and in 1619 the Plaza Mayor was finished.

When the old Alcázar burned on Christmas Eve 1734, the Bourbon king Philip V ordered the stone palace that stands today. His son Charles III, often called Madrid’s best mayor, paved and lit the streets, built the Puerta de Alcalá and planned the building that became the Prado. On 2 May 1808 the people of Madrid rose against Napoleon’s troops, a day that Goya painted, and that began the Peninsular War.

The modern capital

In the 19th century Madrid outgrew its old boundaries, and new districts such as Salamanca rose on a grid to the north and east. From 1910 the city cut the Gran Vía through the old town, and in 1919 the first metro line opened. In the Spanish Civil War Madrid held out for the Republic through a siege of more than two years, until Franco’s troops entered in March 1939.

After Franco’s death in 1975 Madrid became the capital of a democracy and, in the 1980s, the scene of the Movida, a burst of music, film and nightlife. On 11 March 2004 bombs on commuter trains killed nearly 200 people. Today Madrid is Spain’s political and financial centre and one of the largest cities in the European Union.

Madrid today

Madrid is the seat of the king, the government and the parliament, whose lower house, the Congreso de los Diputados, stands on the Carrera de San Jerónimo. Many of Spain’s largest companies and banks have their headquarters here, and along the Paseo del Prado the Prado, the Reina Sofía and the Thyssen-Bornemisza museums lie within a short walk of each other.

Madrileños eat late and spend their evenings outside, on the terraces of the squares and in the tapas bars of La Latina and Huertas. On Sunday mornings the Rastro flea market fills the streets south of La Latina, and in mid-May the city celebrates its patron, San Isidro. Real Madrid plays at the Santiago Bernabéu, Atlético de Madrid at the Metropolitano.
