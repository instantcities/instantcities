Belfort

La tour de 91 m de la ville, commencée en 1313. Gand y gardait ses chartes de privilèges, et des veilleurs y guettaient le feu et l’ennemi. Un dragon doré veille au sommet. Avec la halle aux draps à côté, il est inscrit au patrimoine mondial de l’UNESCO.

The Belfry is the middle one of Ghent’s three towers and, at 91 m, the tallest belfry in Belgium. While the church towers either side speak of God, the Belfry stands for the city itself: for the freedom and the power of its citizens against the count. At the top of the spire turns a gilded dragon, the symbol of Ghent, keeping watch over the city with its head into the wind.

Inside, you climb past the watchmen’s rooms and the halls where the city archive was once kept to the bell floor. There hangs the carillon, one of the largest in the country with over fifty bells, and you can see the huge drum that plays them automatically. From the gallery you get one of the best views over the city: St Bavo’s right next door, the Gravensteen, the Leie with the Graslei and, on a clear day, far beyond Ghent. A lift takes you most of the way up.

Against the tower stands the Cloth Hall, where the cloth merchants had the quality of their woollen cloth inspected: the source of Ghent’s medieval wealth. On the Goudenleeuwplein side is the Mammelokker, the old entrance to the town jail. The sculpture above it shows an old man in his cell being breastfed by his daughter, a story of mercy from Roman antiquity. On the little square beside it stands the cracked bell Triomfante. Histoire

Building began in 1313, when Ghent was, after Paris, one of the largest cities in northern Europe. The builders worked from a design on parchment that still exists and is among the oldest architectural drawings of the Low Countries. By around 1380 the stone tower was standing, with a wooden spire on top. In the ‘secreet’, a strongroom behind thick iron doors, the city kept its privileges: the charters with the rights it had wrested from the counts.

The great bell Roland rang for fire, for war and to call the citizens together. People in Ghent still know the rhyme ‘My name is Roland; when I toll there is fire, when I ring there is a storm in Flanders.’ After the revolt of 1539 the Emperor Charles V punished the city: its privileges were revoked and Roland had to come down. The Hemony bell Triomfante, which later hung in the tower, cracked in 1914 and now stands at the foot of the Belfry.

The Cloth Hall was built against the tower from 1425, but was never quite finished until the early 20th century. The spire too went through many versions: a wooden one, a Baroque one, a cast-iron one from the 19th century and finally today’s, built for the 1913 World’s Fair after the original medieval design. The oldest dragons, from the 14th century, are kept inside the tower.

In 1999 the Belfry, with the Cloth Hall and the Mammelokker, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, among the belfries of Belgium and France. Together these towers tell the story of the free medieval towns, where citizens ran their own city.

Pour aller le voir

The entrance is on the Sint-Baafsplein side, between the cathedral and the town hall. You buy a ticket and go up on foot or by lift; the last stairs to the bells and the gallery are narrow and steep. Come on a clear day for the view if you can, and check whether there is a carillon concert that day: then you hear the bells up close.

Allow about an hour. The lift is a big help if you are not steady on your feet, but not every part of the tower can be reached without stairs.

Plus à Gand

Cathédrale Saint-BavonBâtie du XIIIe au XVIe siècle, avec une tour de 89 m. À l’intérieur se trouve l’Adoration de l’Agneau mystique des frères Van Eyck (1432), l’un des tableaux les plus célèbres et les plus volés du monde. Son panneau des Juges intègres, volé en 1934, n’a jamais été retrouvé. Château des ComtesLe château des comtes de Flandre, bâti en 1180 par Philippe d’Alsace. Il a ensuite servi de tribunal, de prison et même de filature de coton, et a retrouvé ses créneaux vers 1900. En 1949, des étudiants l’ont occupé pour protester contre la hausse du prix de la bière. Graslei et KorenleiLe port médiéval de Gand, sur les deux rives de la Lys. Les bateliers y déchargeaient le grain et les corporations y ont bâti leurs maisons le long du quai. Le Koornstapelhuis, l’entrepôt à grain roman gris d’environ 1200, est l’une des plus vieilles maisons de la ville. Pont Saint-MichelConstruit au début du XXe siècle, juste avant l’Exposition universelle de 1913. Regardez vers l’est pour voir les trois tours de Gand alignées : Saint-Nicolas, le beffroi et Saint-Bavon. Derrière vous se dresse l’église Saint-Michel, dont la tour n’a jamais été achevée. Église Saint-NicolasL’église des marchands, du XIIIe siècle, en pierre bleu-gris de Tournai : du gothique scaldien. Sa tour est posée au milieu du toit et servait de tour de guet, bien avant le beffroi. Elle accueille aujourd’hui aussi des concerts et des expositions. Hôtel de villeUn hôtel de ville en deux styles : gothique tardif fleuri sur la Hoogpoort, Renaissance sobre sur le Botermarkt. Le manque d’argent et les guerres de religion ont sans cesse interrompu les travaux. La Pacification de Gand, une paix entre les provinces des Pays-Bas, y fut signée en 1576. VrijdagmarktLa place des marchés, des fêtes et des révoltes, où les comtes de Flandre prêtaient serment. Au milieu, Jacques van Artevelde, le brasseur qui dirigea Gand en 1338, montre le chemin. En face se dresse Ons Huis, la grande maison du mouvement ouvrier socialiste. Grande BoucherieLa halle couverte des bouchers, bâtie de 1406 à 1419. Des jambons sèchent encore sous son impressionnante charpente de bois. On peut aujourd’hui y acheter et goûter les produits régionaux de Flandre-Orientale. Graffiti StreetThe Werregarenstraatje: since the 1990s anyone may spray paint here, legally. The walls get a fresh layer of paint almost every week, so no two visits look the same. KouterGhent’s smartest square, once a field and a place for parades and archers’ feasts. There has been a flower market here every Sunday since the 18th century, with a brass band in the bandstand. The blue kiosk serves a drink to go with it. VooruitThe party hall of the socialist co-operative Vooruit, opened in 1914: workers came here to dance, for theatre and concerts, and to eat in the café. Artists rescued it from decay in the 1980s. Today it is the arts centre VIERNULVIER. St Peter’s AbbeyThe abbey on the Blandijnberg hill was founded in the 7th century, by Saint Amand as the story goes, and grew into one of the richest in Flanders. The baroque church with its dome (1629-1720) is by Pieter Huyssens. The French dissolved the abbey in 1796; today it is an exhibition hall, with an abbey garden of vines and ruins behind its walls. Gent ZuidUntil 1928 the Zuidstation, Ghent’s first railway station, stood here. Today it is De Lijn’s hub: most city buses start and end here, and the trams pass on their way to the centre and Sint-Pieters. BlaarmeersenGhent’s sports and leisure park, by the Watersportbaan. On hot days the beach on the swimming lake is packed, and joggers run their laps round the 2.3 km rowing course. There is also a campsite, an athletics track and beach volleyball. MammelokkerLook above the door of this little house against the Cloth Hall: a young woman giving an old man her breast. It is the Roman legend of Pero, who nursed her father Cimon when he was left to starve in prison. When it came out, he was set free. The warders of the town prison, which was in the Cloth Hall’s cellars from 1742 to 1902, lived here. Mammelokker is Ghent dialect for breast-sucker. The relief dates from 1741, recarved by Jan Anteunis in 1962. Karel SabbebergGhent’s only mountain, some 30 metres high, and man-made. Until 2003 Ghent skied here on brush mats. In 2019 it was named after the ultrarunner Karel Sabbe, who ran up and down it thousands of times in training. Since 2009 a lookout tower on top looks out over the city. CuberdonsPurple cone-shaped sweets with a runny raspberry heart, sold from little carts on the Groentenmarkt. The tramsDe Lijn’s white and yellow trams, ringing their bells through the narrow streets of the old town. Gentse FeestenTen days every July when the whole old town becomes one of the largest street festivals in Europe. The cycling citySince 2017 cars can no longer drive straight across the centre, and bikes have taken over the streets.
