Graffiti Street

Werregarenstraatje

The Werregarenstraatje: since the 1990s anyone may spray paint here, legally. The walls get a fresh layer of paint almost every week, so no two visits look the same.

Voir en 3D Voir la visite

The Werregarenstraatje is a narrow lane between the Hoogpoort and the Onderstraat, a few steps from the Town Hall and the Vrijdagmarkt. It is only short, but every square centimetre of its walls, doors, gutters and sometimes even the ground is covered in graffiti. It is Belgium’s best-known legal graffiti street, and an open-air gallery that never stays the same.

You find all sorts here: colourful, technically brilliant ‘pieces’ by experienced graffiti artists, quick tags, stencils, stickers, political messages, funny little figures and beginners’ first attempts. Because anyone may spray here, everything is painted over all the time. A work you photograph today may be gone tomorrow. On some walls the layers of paint are by now centimetres thick.

The lane is above all a place to look around, take photos and stop for a moment by the artists you often see at work. It is also an interesting contrast with the medieval buildings right next to it: old and new Ghent a few metres apart. Le visiter en 3D ›

Histoire

The name refers to ‘werregaren’, a kind of yarn or thread that was worked in this area in the Middle Ages: a reminder of the days when Ghent lived from textiles. For a long time it was an ordinary, rather forgotten back street.

In the 1990s the city decided to open the lane to graffiti, as a place where young people and artists could go about it legally. The idea was that there would be less illegal graffiti on historic buildings if there was a place where it was allowed. The lane became a byword in the graffiti world and now draws many tourists too.

Ghent now has more places where graffiti is legal, and the city is known for its street art. Every year new murals appear around town, sometimes by internationally known artists. The Werregarenstraatje remains where that story began.

Pour aller le voir

The Werregarenstraatje is always open and free. You reach it from the Hoogpoort, close to the Town Hall, or from the Onderstraat on the Vrijdagmarkt side. A quick visit takes only a few minutes, but if you look closely you will stay longer.

Want to spray yourself? You may, as long as you respect the neighbours: don’t spray on windows, house doors or parked bikes, and take your empty cans with you.

Plus à Gand

Cathédrale Saint-BavonBâtie du XIIIe au XVIe siècle, avec une tour de 89 m. À l’intérieur se trouve l’Adoration de l’Agneau mystique des frères Van Eyck (1432), l’un des tableaux les plus célèbres et les plus volés du monde. Son panneau des Juges intègres, volé en 1934, n’a jamais été retrouvé. BeffroiLa tour de 91 m de la ville, commencée en 1313. Gand y gardait ses chartes de privilèges, et des veilleurs y guettaient le feu et l’ennemi. Un dragon doré veille au sommet. Avec la halle aux draps à côté, il est inscrit au patrimoine mondial de l’UNESCO. Château des ComtesLe château des comtes de Flandre, bâti en 1180 par Philippe d’Alsace. Il a ensuite servi de tribunal, de prison et même de filature de coton, et a retrouvé ses créneaux vers 1900. En 1949, des étudiants l’ont occupé pour protester contre la hausse du prix de la bière. Graslei et KorenleiLe port médiéval de Gand, sur les deux rives de la Lys. Les bateliers y déchargeaient le grain et les corporations y ont bâti leurs maisons le long du quai. Le Koornstapelhuis, l’entrepôt à grain roman gris d’environ 1200, est l’une des plus vieilles maisons de la ville. Pont Saint-MichelConstruit au début du XXe siècle, juste avant l’Exposition universelle de 1913. Regardez vers l’est pour voir les trois tours de Gand alignées : Saint-Nicolas, le beffroi et Saint-Bavon. Derrière vous se dresse l’église Saint-Michel, dont la tour n’a jamais été achevée. Église Saint-NicolasL’église des marchands, du XIIIe siècle, en pierre bleu-gris de Tournai : du gothique scaldien. Sa tour est posée au milieu du toit et servait de tour de guet, bien avant le beffroi. Elle accueille aujourd’hui aussi des concerts et des expositions. Hôtel de villeUn hôtel de ville en deux styles : gothique tardif fleuri sur la Hoogpoort, Renaissance sobre sur le Botermarkt. Le manque d’argent et les guerres de religion ont sans cesse interrompu les travaux. La Pacification de Gand, une paix entre les provinces des Pays-Bas, y fut signée en 1576. VrijdagmarktLa place des marchés, des fêtes et des révoltes, où les comtes de Flandre prêtaient serment. Au milieu, Jacques van Artevelde, le brasseur qui dirigea Gand en 1338, montre le chemin. En face se dresse Ons Huis, la grande maison du mouvement ouvrier socialiste. Grande BoucherieLa halle couverte des bouchers, bâtie de 1406 à 1419. Des jambons sèchent encore sous son impressionnante charpente de bois. On peut aujourd’hui y acheter et goûter les produits régionaux de Flandre-Orientale. KouterGhent’s smartest square, once a field and a place for parades and archers’ feasts. There has been a flower market here every Sunday since the 18th century, with a brass band in the bandstand. The blue kiosk serves a drink to go with it. VooruitThe party hall of the socialist co-operative Vooruit, opened in 1914: workers came here to dance, for theatre and concerts, and to eat in the café. Artists rescued it from decay in the 1980s. Today it is the arts centre VIERNULVIER. St Peter’s AbbeyThe abbey on the Blandijnberg hill was founded in the 7th century, by Saint Amand as the story goes, and grew into one of the richest in Flanders. The baroque church with its dome (1629-1720) is by Pieter Huyssens. The French dissolved the abbey in 1796; today it is an exhibition hall, with an abbey garden of vines and ruins behind its walls. Gent ZuidUntil 1928 the Zuidstation, Ghent’s first railway station, stood here. Today it is De Lijn’s hub: most city buses start and end here, and the trams pass on their way to the centre and Sint-Pieters. BlaarmeersenGhent’s sports and leisure park, by the Watersportbaan. On hot days the beach on the swimming lake is packed, and joggers run their laps round the 2.3 km rowing course. There is also a campsite, an athletics track and beach volleyball. MammelokkerLook above the door of this little house against the Cloth Hall: a young woman giving an old man her breast. It is the Roman legend of Pero, who nursed her father Cimon when he was left to starve in prison. When it came out, he was set free. The warders of the town prison, which was in the Cloth Hall’s cellars from 1742 to 1902, lived here. Mammelokker is Ghent dialect for breast-sucker. The relief dates from 1741, recarved by Jan Anteunis in 1962. Karel SabbebergGhent’s only mountain, some 30 metres high, and man-made. Until 2003 Ghent skied here on brush mats. In 2019 it was named after the ultrarunner Karel Sabbe, who ran up and down it thousands of times in training. Since 2009 a lookout tower on top looks out over the city. CuberdonsPurple cone-shaped sweets with a runny raspberry heart, sold from little carts on the Groentenmarkt. The tramsDe Lijn’s white and yellow trams, ringing their bells through the narrow streets of the old town. Gentse FeestenTen days every July when the whole old town becomes one of the largest street festivals in Europe. The cycling citySince 2017 cars can no longer drive straight across the centre, and bikes have taken over the streets.
