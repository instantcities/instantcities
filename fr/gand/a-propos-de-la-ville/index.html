Voici Gand

Gand est une ville universitaire de Flandre, le nord néerlandophone de la Belgique, là où la Lys se jette dans l’Escaut. Au Moyen Âge, le commerce du drap en a fait l’une des plus grandes villes au nord des Alpes, et les maisons de corporations de la Graslei, le Château des Comtes et les tours de l’église Saint-Nicolas, du beffroi et de la cathédrale Saint-Bavon montrent encore sa richesse. Saint-Bavon garde le retable de l’Agneau mystique des frères Van Eyck, et depuis 2017 les voitures ne traversent plus le centre médiéval.

La ville

Ghent grew up where the Lys meets the Scheldt, and water still shapes it: rivers and canals wind through the old town, and the medieval harbour on the Lys, between the Graslei and the Korenlei, is the city’s best-known view. Unlike Bruges, Ghent never went to sleep. Its cotton mills made it an industrial city in the 19th century, and today its university fills the streets with students.

Instant Ghent covers the city inside the R40 ring road; its heart is the medieval centre around the old harbour. East of the Lys, St Nicholas’ Church, the Belfry and St Bavo’s Cathedral stand in a line, and the Veldstraat leads south from the Korenmarkt to the shops. North of the river the Gravensteen guards the narrow lanes of the Patershol, and a few streets further east lies the Vrijdagmarkt, for centuries the square where the people of Ghent met to rise against their rulers.

Où elle se trouve

Ghent lies in the north-west of Belgium, in the Flemish Region, and is the capital of the province of East Flanders. It lies about 55 kilometres north-west of Brussels and 50 kilometres south-west of Antwerp, on flat land where the Lys (Leie) joins the Scheldt (Schelde).

A canal runs north from Ghent to Terneuzen on the Western Scheldt, in the Netherlands, so that sea ships can reach the city’s port; since 2018 that port has been part of North Sea Port, which Ghent shares with the Dutch ports of Zeeland. Ghent’s climate is mild and damp, like the rest of Flanders: cool summers, soft winters and rain in every season.

Lieux et monuments

Cathédrale Saint-BavonBâtie du XIIIe au XVIe siècle, avec une tour de 89 m. À l’intérieur se trouve l’Adoration de l’Agneau mystique des frères Van Eyck (1432), l’un des tableaux les plus célèbres et les plus volés du monde. Son panneau des Juges intègres, volé en 1934, n’a jamais été retrouvé. BeffroiLa tour de 91 m de la ville, commencée en 1313. Gand y gardait ses chartes de privilèges, et des veilleurs y guettaient le feu et l’ennemi. Un dragon doré veille au sommet. Avec la halle aux draps à côté, il est inscrit au patrimoine mondial de l’UNESCO. Château des ComtesLe château des comtes de Flandre, bâti en 1180 par Philippe d’Alsace. Il a ensuite servi de tribunal, de prison et même de filature de coton, et a retrouvé ses créneaux vers 1900. En 1949, des étudiants l’ont occupé pour protester contre la hausse du prix de la bière.
Graslei et KorenleiLe port médiéval de Gand, sur les deux rives de la Lys. Les bateliers y déchargeaient le grain et les corporations y ont bâti leurs maisons le long du quai. Le Koornstapelhuis, l’entrepôt à grain roman gris d’environ 1200, est l’une des plus vieilles maisons de la ville. Pont Saint-MichelConstruit au début du XXe siècle, juste avant l’Exposition universelle de 1913. Regardez vers l’est pour voir les trois tours de Gand alignées : Saint-Nicolas, le beffroi et Saint-Bavon. Derrière vous se dresse l’église Saint-Michel, dont la tour n’a jamais été achevée. Église Saint-NicolasL’église des marchands, du XIIIe siècle, en pierre bleu-gris de Tournai : du gothique scaldien. Sa tour est posée au milieu du toit et servait de tour de guet, bien avant le beffroi. Elle accueille aujourd’hui aussi des concerts et des expositions. Hôtel de villeUn hôtel de ville en deux styles : gothique tardif fleuri sur la Hoogpoort, Renaissance sobre sur le Botermarkt. Le manque d’argent et les guerres de religion ont sans cesse interrompu les travaux. La Pacification de Gand, une paix entre les provinces des Pays-Bas, y fut signée en 1576. VrijdagmarktLa place des marchés, des fêtes et des révoltes, où les comtes de Flandre prêtaient serment. Au milieu, Jacques van Artevelde, le brasseur qui dirigea Gand en 1338, montre le chemin. En face se dresse Ons Huis, la grande maison du mouvement ouvrier socialiste. Grande BoucherieLa halle couverte des bouchers, bâtie de 1406 à 1419. Des jambons sèchent encore sous son impressionnante charpente de bois. On peut aujourd’hui y acheter et goûter les produits régionaux de Flandre-Orientale.

Les rues, les places et les cours d’eau où finit tout visiteur de Gand. Pointez-en un pour le trouver sur la carte.

    Graslei rue

    The quay on the east bank of the old harbour, lined with the guild houses of the medieval merchants and boatmen.

  2. 2
    Korenlei rue

    The quay across the water from the Graslei, with merchants’ houses of the 16th to 18th centuries.

  3. 3
    The Lys Leie cours d’eau

    The river that flows through the old town to meet the Scheldt. Its stretch between the bridges was Ghent’s medieval harbour; today tour boats leave from its quays.

  4. 4
    St Michael’s Bridge Sint-Michielsbrug pont

    The stone bridge over the Lys from which you see the Graslei, the Korenlei and the three towers of the old town in a row.

  5. 5
    Korenmarkt place

    The old corn market, a long square at the foot of St Nicholas’ Church, where trams pass and the Veldstraat begins.

  6. 6
    Emile Braunplein place

    The open square between St Nicholas’ Church and the Belfry, with the timber and glass city pavilion, the Stadshal.

  7. 7
    Sint-Baafsplein place

    The square in front of St Bavo’s Cathedral, with the Dutch Theatre (NTGent) facing it.

  8. 8
    Botermarkt place

    The old butter market, faced by the Renaissance front of the City Hall; its Gothic side looks onto the Hoogpoort.

  9. 9
    Werregarenstraat rue

    A narrow alley where graffiti is allowed, painted over again and again.

  10. 10
    Vrijdagmarkt place

    The big Friday market square, for centuries the place of meetings and revolts, with a statue of Jacob van Artevelde.

  11. 11
    Kraanlei rue

    A quay along the Lys with old gabled houses, named after the crane that once unloaded the boats here.

  12. 12
    Patershol quartier

    A maze of medieval lanes behind the Kraanlei, once a poor quarter of craftsmen, now full of restaurants.

  13. 13
    Groentenmarkt place

    The old vegetable market beside the long roof of the Groot Vleeshuis, the medieval meat hall.

  14. 14
    Sint-Veerleplein place

    The square in front of the Gravensteen, once the place of the town’s public executions.

  15. 15
    Veldstraat rue

    Ghent’s main shopping street, running south from the Korenmarkt, free of cars.

Histoire

Abbeys and counts

Around 630 the missionary St Amand founded two abbeys where the Lys meets the Scheldt: St Bavo’s and, on the Blandijn hill, St Peter’s. A trading town grew between them, and its name probably comes from a Celtic word for a meeting of rivers. In 879 a Viking army made its winter camp at St Bavo’s Abbey.

The counts of Flanders built a castle by the Lys to protect the town, and in 1180 Count Philip of Alsace rebuilt it in stone as the Gravensteen. Ghent grew rich on cloth, woven from English wool, and by 1300 it was one of the largest cities north of the Alps. The merchants built their guild houses along the harbour on the Graslei and the city raised the Belfry as the tower of its freedom.

Rebels and an emperor

Ghent’s weavers and merchants often defied their counts and the kings of France. In 1338 Jacob van Artevelde took charge of the city and sided with England in the Hundred Years’ War, to keep the English wool coming. In 1432 Hubert and Jan van Eyck finished the Ghent Altarpiece for the church of St John, today St Bavo’s Cathedral.

Charles V, who would rule half of Europe, was born in Ghent’s Prinsenhof in 1500. When his native city refused to pay his taxes and revolted, he came back in 1540, took away its privileges and made its leading citizens beg for mercy barefoot, with nooses around their necks; the people of Ghent still call themselves the noose-bearers, the stroppendragers. In 1576 the Pacification of Ghent briefly united the Low Countries against the Spanish troops of his son, Philip II.

Manchester of the Continent

Around 1800 Lieven Bauwens smuggled a spinning machine out of England and set up machine spinning in Ghent. Cotton and flax mills followed, and the city became the most industrial in Belgium, the ‘Manchester of the Continent’, with crowded workers’ quarters and a strong socialist movement. In 1814 British and American diplomats signed the Treaty of Ghent here to end the War of 1812, and in 1817 King William I founded the university.

Ghent held a World’s Fair in 1913, and in 1930 its university became the first in Belgium to teach in Dutch. When the textile mills closed in the 20th century, the port, a steel works and a car factory took their place. The old centre, long grey with soot, was cleaned and restored, and in 2017 the city closed it to through traffic.

Gand aujourd’hui

Ghent is the second-largest city of Flanders, after Antwerp. Its port, its steel works and its car factory still employ thousands, but the university and its tens of thousands of students set the tone of the city. The town council has pushed cars out of the centre and bicycles into it, and in 2009 it became one of the first cities to promote a meat-free Thursday.

Every July the Gentse Feesten fill the squares of the old town with free concerts and street theatre for ten days. Ghent people eat waterzooi, a creamy stew of chicken or fish, and buy cuberdons, the purple ‘noses’ of raspberry syrup, from carts on the Groentenmarkt.
