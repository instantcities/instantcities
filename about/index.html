The idea

Most of us know a city from a map, from photos or from one short visit. Instant Cities lets you ride through it instead: along its streets, over its bridges and across its squares, at your own pace, on a bike, on foot or on a horse. You can look around before a trip, find your way back to a street you remember, or race a friend through an old town neither of you has seen.

We keep the barrier as low as we can. Every city runs in an ordinary browser, on a laptop, a tablet or a phone. You don't install anything, you don't make an account, and you don't need a fast graphics card. A link to a city drops you into it, and a link to a landmark drops you right in front of it.

Cities built from OpenStreetMap

Every city starts from OpenStreetMap, the free map of the world that volunteers keep up to date. Our tools download a city's historic centre from it and turn what they find into a 3D world: the outline and height of every building, the streets and their surfaces (cobbles where OpenStreetMap says so), the bridges, quays, canals and rivers, the squares, parks and trees, and the tram lines.

OpenStreetMap gives us the shape of a city, but not always its look. So we add to it by hand: landmarks such as belfries, cathedrals and bridges get a model of their own, and in Ghent we colour the house fronts one street at a time after street-level photos from Mapillary and Wikimedia Commons. Ghent's buildings even stand at their measured height, taken from Flanders' open height model of the region.

Ghent and Lecce are built in full. For the other cities we have built a first piece of the centre, with a race and a tour, and we grow them step by step.

A game in your browser, with three.js

The game draws its cities with three.js, the JavaScript library that puts WebGL graphics in a web page. It is written as plain JavaScript modules that the browser loads as they are: there is no build step and no app store. The whole site is a set of static files that GitHub Pages serves, so a new version goes live as soon as we push it.

In every city you can ride about freely and look, race along real streets against the clock or against a second player on the same keyboard, play short quests, or sit back and let the tour fly you past the sights. You pick a bike, your own feet or a horse, or a cow, a tiger, a pig or a unicorn: they are all equally fast and strong, so the choice is only about looks.

To run on a phone the game keeps things light: it loads a city in steps while the loading screen tells you what it is doing, and it turns down its detail on touch screens. Once you have played a city, your browser keeps a copy of it, so it opens faster the next time and even starts without a network.

Made with Claude and AI

Instant Cities is vibe-coded: we describe what we want, and AI writes most of the code. Most of that work happens with Anthropic's Claude, in Claude Code, and some with OpenAI. It writes the tools that turn OpenStreetMap into a city, the builders for bridges, towers and quays, the game itself and this website, and it helps write the guides and landmark pages.

The AI also checks its own work. It opens the game in a browser, takes screenshots of a street or a tour, compares them with photos of the real place, and fixes what lies wrong: a bridge that sits too low, a path that cuts through a wall, a house front in the wrong colour.

The people behind the project, Wouter and Julia, decide what to build, ride through the cities and point out what still looks off. Without AI a project like this would take a team years. With it, the first pieces of six new cities came together in a single day.

It started with Instant Gent

The project began as Instant Gent, a 3D game of Ghent in Belgium, made to show the city's streets, canals and towers to anyone with a browser. Instant Gent still lives on its own site, in Dutch, French and Spanish, with an app for the iPhone.

Once Ghent worked, we wanted to know whether the same tools could build any old town, and Lecce, in the south of Italy, became the first city after Ghent. In October 2026 we split the game off into Instant Cities, took Ghent and Lecce along, and started on Rome, Paris, London, Venice and the other cities you can play here.

Open source, on GitHub

All of the code is public at github.com/instantcities/instantcities: the game, the tools that build a city from OpenStreetMap, each city's own models and data, and this website. You can read how a city comes together, run it on your own computer, or open an issue when a street, a bridge or a landmark looks wrong.

If something on the map is wrong in OpenStreetMap itself, the best fix is to correct it there: then every map that uses OpenStreetMap gets better, and our city does too the next time we build it.

Where it could go

We are still working out where this goes, but here is one idea. Each city could get an open source repository of its own on GitHub, kept up by the people who know and love it: they would correct its streets, model its landmarks, colour its house fronts and write about its places.

Those repositories would grow into a data source in their own right, next to OpenStreetMap: a free, open 3D model of each historic centre that anyone could use, not only this game. We haven't decided anything yet. If you would like to help shape it, get in touch through GitHub.
